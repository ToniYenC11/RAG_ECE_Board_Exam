## MATERIAL 6 -FUELS

## 4.1 Fuels

Fuels are materials that store potential energy in forms and liberate heat energy upon burning with oxygen. The heat energy released by reactions of fuels is converted into mechanical energy via a heat engine. The combustion of wood or sticks was the first use of fuels million years ago. Charcoal was also used during the early times. For machines, hydrocarbons and coal are the most commonly used fuels.

An ideal fuel should possess the following characteristics:

- High calorific value
- Moderate ignition temperature
- Low moisture content
- Low non-combustible matter
- Moderate velocity of combustion
- Products of combustion not harmful
- Low cost
- Easy to transport
- Combustion should be controllable
- No spontaneous combustion
- Low storage cost
- Should burn in air with efficiency

## 4.2 Types of Fuel

There are three types of fuel: solid, liquid and gaseous fuels. The type of fuels depends on their state.

- a.  Solid fuel - has both definite shape and volume. Wood, coal and charcoal are known examples of this.
- b.  Liquid  fuel -  also  occupies  a  constant  amount  of  space  (has  definite volume),  but  its  forms  change  readily  (has  indefinite  shape).  Examples  of liquid fuel are petroleum, diesel, gasoline, kerosene, LPG and ethanol.
- c.  Gaseous fuel - has neither definite volume nor definite shape. This includes natural gas, hydrogen, propane, methane and coal gas.

## 3 Alternative Sources of Fuels

The following are the different alternative sources of fuels:

- Biodiesel  is  a  renewable  fuel  that  can  be  manufactured  from  vegetable  oils, animal fats, or recycled cooking oils for use in diesel-driven vehicles
- Hydrogen is a potentially emission- free alternative fuel that can be produced  for use in fuel cell vehicles.
- Electricity can be used to power plug-in electric vehicles, which is popularly used nowadays.
- Ethanol  is  a  widely  used  renewable  fuel  made  from  corn  and  other  plant materials. It is blended with gasoline for use in vehicles.
- Propane is a readily available gaseous fuel that has been widely used in vehicles throughout the world for some time.
- Photovoltaic  cells  convert  light  directly  into  electrical  energy,  thus,  power  up vehicles.
- Nuclear fuels are materials that produce nuclear energy through nuclear fission or nuclear fusion.
- Calorific value , or the heat of combustion, is the amount of heat produced when 1  gram  of  fuel  is  burnt.  When  1  gram  of  methane  undergoes  complete combustion, 15 kilojoules of heat is liberated, then the calorific value of methane is 15 kJ/gram. When 1 gram of propane is completely burnt, 10 kilojoules of heat is produced, then the calorific value of propane is 10 kJ/gram.
- Higher  calorific  value means  the  total  quantity  of  heat  liberated  from combustion of a unit mass or unit volume of  given fuel when by-products/side products  are  allowed  to  cool  at  room  temperature  and  the  amount  of  heat released by the water vapor is recovered.
- Lower  calorific  value means  the  total  quantity  of  heat  liberated  from combustion of a unit mass or unit volume of given fuel when side products (ash, volatile materials) are allowed to escape and the amount of heat released by the water vapor is not recovered.

The lower calorific value could be calculated as:

Low Calorific Value (LCV) = High Calorific Value (HCV) - amount of heat carried by steam

Amount of heat carried by steam = 44 kJ/mol

4.7 Fuel Consumption and Carbon Dioxide Emission

The amount of CO2 a vehicle emits is directly related to the amount/type of fuel it consumes.  For  instance,  a  diesel  car  emitting  95g  CO2  per  kilometre  consumes around 3.7 litres of fuel per 100km, while a petrol car consumes around 4 litres/100km for the same CO2 emissions.

Fuel economy also referred to as fuel efficiency, is the relationship between the distance travelled and the fuel consumed.

## 1.  Diesel:

1  liter  of  diesel  weighs  835  grams.  Diesel  consists  of  86.2%  carbon,  or  720 grams of carbon per liter diesel. In order to combust this carbon to CO2, 1920

grams  of  oxygen  is  needed.  The  sum  is  then  720  +  1920  =  2640  grams  of CO2/liter diesel.

An average consumption of 5 liters/100 km then corresponds to 5 L x 2640 g/L /100 per km = 132 g CO2/km.

## 2.  Petrol:

1 liter of petrol weighs 750 grams. Petrol consists of 87% carbon, or 652 grams of carbon per liter of petrol. In order to combust this carbon to CO2, 1740 grams of oxygen is needed. The sum is then 652 + 1740 = 2392 grams of CO2/liter of petrol.

An average consumption of 5 liters/100 km then corresponds to 5 L x 2392 g/L/ 100 per km  =   120 g. CO2 /km.

## 3.  LPG:

1 liter of LPG weighs 550 grams. LPG consists of 82.5% carbon, or 454 grams of carbon per liter of LPG. In order to combust this carbon to CO2, 1211 grams of oxygen is  needed.  The  sum  is  then  454  +  1211  =  1665  grams  of  CO2/liter  of LPG.

An average consumption of 5 liters/ 100 km then corresponds to

5L x 1665 g/L / 100 per km = 83 g of CO2/km
