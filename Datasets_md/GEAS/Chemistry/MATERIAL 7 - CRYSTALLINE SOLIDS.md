## MATERIAL 7 -CRYSTALLINE SOLIDS

## 7.1 Crystalline Solids

Solids  can  be  classified  based  on  the  arrangement  of  particles;  they  can  be either crystalline or amorphous solids.

If the particles are arranged and organized in a specific location, or form rigid and long-range  order,  the  solids  are crystalline .  Crystalline  solids  have  symmetrical structures, and thus when cut, flat and smooth surfaces can be seen. They cleave along particular direction at fixed cleavage  planes.  Also,  because  of  definite  packing, crystalline solids have sharp melting points and heat of fusions.   Example of crystalline solid is salt.

## Types of Crystalline Solids

The properties of crystalline solid depend on the forces of attraction including the electrostatic  attraction,  covalent  bond,  intermolecular  forces  (dipole-dipole,  dispersion and hydrogen bond) and metallic bond.

- Ionic crystals - are ionic compounds which are formed by actual electron-transfer where one of the reacting atoms loses one or more electrons and the other atom gains one or more electrons. The metal atom on losing electrons form a positive ion called cation and the nonmetal atom gaining electrons forms a negative ion called anion. Examples are NaCl, KCl, KI, MgCl2, BaCl2. The ions in ionic compounds are held  by  very  strong  electrostatic  forces  (stronger  than  intermolecular  forces). Because of this, ionic compounds are hard, have high melting point and high boiling point.  Solid  ionic  compounds  do  not  conduct  electricity  solid  unless  they  are dissolved in water.
- Covalent  crystals -  are  solids  in  which  atoms  are  bonded  by  strong  covalent bonds. Covalent bond is formed by electron sharing, usually between atoms of nonmetals.  Examples  are  diamond  and  graphite.    In  diamond,  each  carbon  atom  is covalently  bonded  to  four  other  carbon  atoms  forming  a  tetrahedral  geometry. Diamond is the strongest mineral because it forms an extensive three dimensional networks which are difficult to fracture. Similarly, graphite contains carbon similar to diamond, however, the atoms are held together by weaker covalent bonds, making it  soft compared to diamond.   The three-dimensional network is a flat, hexagonal sheet that is stacked together.  The sheet can slide across one another, and thus is slippery to touch.
- Molecular crystals -are molecules held by dipole-dipole (between polar molecules), dipole-induced dipole (between polar and nonpolar substance), dispersion  (all  substances)  and  hydrogen  bonding  (in  substances  containing hydrogen attached to O, N, and F). For the reason that these forces are weak as compared with ionic and covalent bonds, molecular crystals are soft and have low melting  point.  Furthermore,  they  are  poor  conductor  of  heat  and  electricity. Examples are H2O(s) (hydrogen bonding, dipole-dipole, dispersion), CO2 (dispersion),  C12H22O11  (dipole-dipole,  hydrogen  bonding,  dispersion),  and  SO2 (dispersion).
- Metallic crystals - in a metallic crystal, the principal constituent particles are atoms of  the  same metal. Usually, metals have one to four outer electrons so there are empty orbitals in the last energy level. Since the atoms are closely packed, these orbitals  overlap  allowing  loosely  bonded  electrons  to  move  from  one  atom,  to another resulting into a 'sea of electrons'.

The mobile electron cloud acts as binding force holding the positive metal ions together. Having a close-packed crystal structure indicates that the attractive force is  strong,  making  the  metal  ductile,  malleable,  lustrous,  dense,  and  with  high melting  point.  The  mobile  electrons  make  the  metal  good  conductor  of  heat  and electricity. Examples of metallic crystals are silver, copper, chromium, and tungsten.

## 7.2 Crystal Lattices

There are many ways to arrange atoms to form crystalline solid, however no matter the arrangements are, there are always gaps between them. The more gaps indicate lower packing efficiency and density. Packing efficiency represents the percentage of space that is occupied in a given arrangement. Aside from the packing efficiency, crystal lattices differ in terms of the number of atoms per unit cell and coordination number. In the former, the number of atoms that is included in the unit cell only includes the fractions of atoms inside of the box. Atoms on the corners of the unit cell count as ⅛ of an atom, atoms on a face count as ½, an atom in the center counts as a full atom. On the other hand, coordination number is the number of atoms immediately adjacent to any given atom. By increasing the coordination numbers, they allow each atom in the lattice to interact  with  more  nearest  neighbor  atoms.  The  attractive  forces  of  those  interactions  are ultimately responsible for holding the crystal together.

## Four basic crystal structures

- a. Simple  -  Cubic  Cell  (SCC) -  in  this  structure, each  sphere  is  in  contact  with  each  other.  The second layer is directly over the first layer sphere. Each sphere in this structure touches four identical spheres  in  the  same  plane.  It  also  touches  one sphere  in  the  plane  above  and  one  in  the  plane below. Each atom in this structure can form bonds to  its  six  nearest  neighbors,  thus  the  coordination number  is  6.  Simple-cubic  cell  is  the  simplest looking  arrangement,  it  is  also  the  least  efficient way  to  fill  space.  The  packing  efficiency  is  only 52%. Because this structure is inefficient, only one element  polonium  crystallizes  in  a  simple  cubic structure.

One unit cell of simple cubic packing has eight corners,  thus  the  number  of  atoms  per  unit  cell  is  1 since atoms on the corners of the unit cell count as ⅛ of atom. The relationship between the edge length (a) and the radius (r) is a = 2r.

Face-Centered  Cubic  Cell  (FCC)  or  Cubic  Close Packing (CCP) -  this  structure  contains  atoms,  each  with  coordination  number of 12, six in its own layer, three in the layer above, and three in the layer below. FCC can be formed by forming a hexagon layer (plane) in which each atom touches other atoms.  The  atoms  in  the  second  layer  are  then  placed  above  the  triangular depression in the first layer, and the atoms in the third layer are positioned above the depression in the first layer that was not utilized to form the second layer. The fourth hexagonal layer of atoms are placed directly above the first. This forms an ABCABCABC . . . structure.

Face-centered cubic is called as such for the reasons that one atom is located at each corner of the cube, and additional atoms are placed at the center of each of the six faces of the cube. The atoms at the four corners of a face all 'touch' the atom at the center of that face, but the atoms at the corners do not touch one another.

A unit cell has eight corners and six faces, giving six face-center atoms and eight corner atoms, thus the number of atoms per unit cell is 4 since atoms on the corners of the unit cell count as ⅛ of an atom and atoms on a face count as ½.

## 5.3 X-ray Diffraction

All we know about crystal structures has been learned from X-ray diffraction studies. Xray diffraction refers to the scattering of X rays by the unit of a crystalline solid. The scattering, or  diffraction,  patterns  produced  are  used  to  deduce the  arrangement of particles in the solid lattice.

In 1912, German physicist Max von Laue suggested that, because the wavelength of Xrays is comparable in magnitude to the distances between lattice points in a crystal, the lattice should be able to diffract X-rays.
