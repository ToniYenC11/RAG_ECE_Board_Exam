## THERMOCHEMISTRY

The system may be open, closed, or isolated. In an open system, there is an exchange of  matter  and  energy  while  in  a closed  system ,  there  is  an  exchange  of  energy  but  no exchange  of  matter.  On  the  other  hand,  if  there  is  no  exchange  of  matter  and  energy,  the system is said to be isolated . The  transfer  of  energy  is  either  an  exothermic  or  endothermic  process.  For  example, burning of wood wherein the wood is part of the system is an exothermic process. As it burns, we feel warm while sitting beside it. Our body is part of the surroundings and the heat produced by  the  burning  wood  is  transferred  to  our  body.  This  process  of  transferring  heat  from  the process. If the flow of heat is from the system to the surroundings is known as an exothermic surroundings to the system, it is known as endothermic process.

## Heat and Temperature

Heat is a form of energy. It transfers between a system and its surroundings because of a  difference  in  temperature  between  them.  Natural  flow  of  heat  is  from  a  region  of  higher temperature  to  the  region  of  lower  temperature  until  the  two  regions  reach  the  same temperature. However, if the flow of heat is from the region of lower temperature to the region of higher temperature, work is applied.

Temperature ,  which  is  a  measure  of  the  average  kinetic  energies  of  the  atoms  and molecules of a substance indicates the direction of the flow of heat. When the particles collide at the interface between the system and its surroundings, the more energetic particles (those at higher  temperature)  lose  energy  to  the  less  energetic  particles  (those  at  lower  temperature). The  transfer  of  kinetic  energy  stops  when  a  system  and  its  surroundings  are  at  the  same temperature.

Traditionally, energy is expressed in calories. This unit of energy changes is defined as the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1.00 g of water 1.0 degree Celsius (from 14.5 ºC to  15.5  ºC).  One  calorie  is  also  equal to  4.184 Joule  (the  SI  unit  of  energy  change)  and  one joule  is  equivalent  to  1  kg·m 2 /s 2 .  But  since  Joule  (J)  is  a  small  unit,  energy  associated  with chemical reactions are often expressed in kilo joule (kJ).

## First Law of Thermodynamics

The First Law of Thermodynamics states that energy can neither be created nor destroyed but only converted from one form to another . The First Law is used to both measure and calculate internal energy and enthalpy changes for reactions.

The energy of the system is called internal energy (U) .  It  is  the  sum  of the  microscopic  kinetic  energy  (the  disordered  microscopic  motion  of  atoms  and molecules)  and  microscopic  potential  energy  (associated  with  molecular  attractive forces) of the system.

Internal energy can be transferred between a system and its surroundings in the form of either heat (q) or work (w).

## EXAMPLE

What is the change in the internal energy of a system that absorbs 455 J of heat and does 325 J of work?

Given: Since the system absorbs heat, q = +455 J

and does work, w = -325 J

Required: internal energy

Solution:

∆U = q + w

$$= +455 J + (-325J) = +130 J$$

The internal energy of the system increases since more heat is absorbed than the work done.

## Hess' law

Hess' Law is used to calculate ∆H value for reactions which is impossible to do in one step. It states that the overall ∆H for a chemical reaction can be determined by summing up  the  ∆Hs  of  the  elementary  reactions  that  make  up  the  overall  reaction. Another way to look at it is to remember that ∆H is a state property, so it is must be independent of the path of a reaction.

<!-- formula-not-decoded -->

Rules for using Hess' Law

1.  If  the reaction is multiplied (or divided) by some factor, ΔH must also be multiplied (or divided) by that same factor
2.  If the reaction is reversed (flipped), the sign of ΔH must also be reversed.

## Standard Enthalpy of Formation,

Aside  from  Hess's  Law,  enthalpy  change  can  be  determined  using  the  actual enthalpies  of  formation of  each  of  the  substances  present  in  the  thermochemical reaction.

Enthalpy of formation is the enthalpy associated with the reaction that forms one mole of a compound from its elements in their most thermodynamically stable states. These are measured on a relative scale and for elements, the enthalpy of formation is equal to zero.

The standard enthalpy of formation (∆H°f) is the enthalpy change, ∆H, when one mole of the compound is formed from the elements in their stable forms at 25 °C and 1 atmosphere. The standard enthalpy of formation can be used to determine the standard enthalpy of reaction (∆Hºrxn). Enthalpy of reaction is equal to the sum of the enthalpies of the products minus the sum of the enthalpies of the reactants.
